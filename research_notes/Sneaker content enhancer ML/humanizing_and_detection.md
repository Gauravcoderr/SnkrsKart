## Q1. How detectors work, their real accuracy and false-positive rates, and whether Google uses one

### Takeaway
Three detector families exist: zero-shot perplexity methods (DetectGPT, Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars), supervised classifiers (Pangram, GPTZero, Originality.ai, Turnitin) and provider-side watermarks (SynthID-Text). On curated benchmarks the best supervised classifier (Pangram) reaches near-zero error, but every family degrades sharply under paraphrase or "humanizer" rewriting, zero-shot methods over-flag simpler or non-native prose, and independent 2026 field data show 9 to 15% of untouched human academic abstracts being flagged. Google states it judges quality and scaled-abuse patterns, not production method, and its documentation never mentions running an AI detector.

### Cited Findings

Zero-shot (perplexity-based) methods
- Binoculars (Hans et al., ICML 2024) scores text as log-perplexity divided by log cross-perplexity between an "observer" and a "performer" model (Falcon-7B / Falcon-7B-Instruct), needs no training data, and reports 95% (News), 99% (Creative Writing) and 98% (Student Essay) true-positive rate at 0.01% false-positive rate on ChatGPT text, beating GPTZero, DetectGPT and Ghostbuster. [peer-reviewed] - [Binoculars, arXiv 2401.12070v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.12070v3)
- Binoculars reports 99.67% accuracy on ESL (non-native) essays, while the competing detectors it cites flagged non-native writing as machine-generated 48 to 76% of the time; it also admits false positives on memorised text (the US Constitution scores 0.76, below its 0.901 machine threshold). [peer-reviewed] - [Binoculars](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.12070v3)
- On 300 real non-fiction articles generated by GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet and o1-Pro (Russell et al., ACL 2025), zero-shot detectors did far worse than their benchmark numbers: Fast-DetectGPT 80.0% TPR / 7.2% FPR, Binoculars 66.7% TPR / 1.3% FPR, RADAR 15.3% TPR / 2% FPR. Under GPT-4o paraphrasing Binoculars fell to 60.0% TPR / 6.7% FPR; under o1-Pro "humanisation" prompting Binoculars fell to 6.7% TPR and Fast-DetectGPT to 23.3% TPR. [peer-reviewed] - [Russell et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v1); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267)

Supervised commercial classifiers
- Same Russell et al. study: Pangram 98.0% TPR / 2% FPR, Pangram "Humanizers" model 99.3% TPR / 2.7% FPR, GPTZero 85.3% TPR / 0.7% FPR. Under humanisation Pangram Humanizers kept 96.7% TPR but FPR rose to 10%. [peer-reviewed] - [Russell et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v1)
- University of Chicago BFI working paper 2025-116 (Jabarian and Imas, Aug/Oct 2025): 1,992 human passages in six genres (news, blogs, consumer reviews, novels, restaurant reviews, résumés) matched with text from GPT-4.1, Claude Opus 4, Claude Sonnet 4 and Gemini 2.0 Flash. Pangram achieved "essentially zero" FPR and FNR; the open-source RoBERTa baseline had 30 to 78% FPR; Pangram was the only detector meeting a 0.5% FPR policy cap while still detecting; "minimizing false positive rate favors GPTZero, while maximizing ability [to detect] ... OriginalityAI". Under the StealthGPT humanizer GPTZero's false-negative rate rose to "around 50% and above across most genres", while Pangram stayed low. [working paper, not yet peer-reviewed] - [BFI research brief PDF](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Artificial-Writing-and-Automated-Detection.pdf); [BFI working paper page](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/working-papers/artificial-writing-and-automated-detection/)
- Pangram's own curated list of third-party evaluations adds a Vrije Universiteit Brussel paper (June 2026, 160 academic papers of 4000+ words incl. ESL text; Pangram 97.5% detection on fully-AI text, 0% false positives; GPTZero, Turnitin and Copyleaks also tested, "only [Pangram] produced satisfactory results"). [vendor-curated, cites peer-reviewed] - [Pangram third-party evals](https://www.pangram.com/blog/third-party-pangram-evals); Pangram's own method description is in its technical report. [vendor] - [arXiv 2402.14873](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14873v3)
- Field reality check (Karr, Khvatskii, Hua, Chawla, Notre Dame, arXiv Aug 2026): on published academic abstracts, unmodified 2023 to 2025 originals are flagged at 9 to 15% by commercial detectors (they call these "flag rates, not confirmed false positives" because abstracts may already contain AI edits), guideline-compliant light AI edits are flagged at 38 to 80% (Pangram 64 to 80%, GPTZero 38 to 49%), and after Undetectable AI v11 humanisation fewer than 4% of AI rewrites remain flagged (FNR > 96%, n = 2,568). Flag rates vary by field: Pangram 2.1% in chemistry vs 26.6% in theology, GPTZero 1.1% to 15.2%. The paper notes "Vendor marketing claims sub-1% false-positive rates on benchmark" data. [preprint] - [arXiv 2608.11256](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11256)
- "Style as a Confound" (Park, Jeong, Kim, EMNLP 2026): 135,389 pairs of non-native manuscripts and their professionally edited versions run through 13 detectors; FPRs on human text ranged "from 0.0% to 100.0%" across detectors, and identical professional edits raised AI scores in some tools and lowered them in others, identifying editing style itself as a confound. [peer-reviewed] - [arXiv 2608.26710](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26710)
- Liang et al. (Patterns, July 2023): seven detectors misclassified TOEFL essays by non-native writers as AI at an average 61.3% false-positive rate while correctly classifying US eighth-grade essays; prompting ChatGPT to enrich vocabulary reduced misclassification, simplifying language increased it. [peer-reviewed] - [arXiv 2304.02819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819); [Times Higher Education summary](https://timeshighereducation.com/cn/news/ai-text-detectors-biased-against-non-native-english-speakers)
- Turnitin's own figures: document-level FPR under 1% when a document is 20%+ AI, 4% sentence-level FPR. [vendor] - [Turnitin blog](https://www.turnitin.co.uk/blog/understanding-the-false-positive-rate-for-sentences-of-our-ai-writing-detection-capability). Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's detector in Aug 2023, reasoning that on its 75,000 papers a year a sub-1% rate still implies roughly 750 wrongly flagged papers, and citing bias against ESL students. [institutional statement] - [Vanderbilt](https://www.vanderbilt.edu/brightspace/2023/08/16/guidance-on-ai-detection-and-why-were-disabling-turnitins-ai-detector/)
- Originality.ai claims 99%+ accuracy for its Turbo model on OpenAI, Gemini, Claude and DeepSeek output and 99% accuracy with 0.5% FPR for Lite. [vendor] - [Originality.ai](https://originality.ai/blog/ai-accuracy). Independent review-site tests put real-world accuracy at 76 to 92% with FPRs of roughly 4.8 to 5.7%. [secondary, not peer-reviewed] - [fast.io comparison](https://www.fast.io/resources/ai-detector-accuracy-comparison-2026.md)
- GPTZero is publicly described as scoring perplexity plus "burstiness" (variation in sentence length and structure). Review-site testing reports 99.5% on the Chicago benchmark but about 87% with 10% FPR on a 2,400-sample mixed set, and notes that newer models have "largely closed the gap in predictability". [secondary] - [fast.io GPTZero review](https://www.fast.io/resources/gptzero-ai-detector-review-2026/)

Robustness benchmarks and attacks
- RAID (Dugan et al., ACL 2024): 6M+ generations, 11 generators, 8 domains, 11 adversarial attacks, 4 decoding strategies. Detectors "are easily fooled by adversarial attacks, variations in sampling strategies, repetition penalties, and unseen generative models". Hardest attacks: homoglyph (49.2% TPR), paraphrase (60.6%), synonym swap (61.3%). [peer-reviewed] - [RAID](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07940)
- DIPPER paraphraser (Krishna et al., NeurIPS 2023) cut DetectGPT from 70.3% to 4.6% detection at 1% FPR and also evaded watermarking, GPTZero and OpenAI's classifier; a retrieval defence over 15M stored generations recovered 80 to 97% detection at 1% FPR. [peer-reviewed] - [DIPPER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13408)
- SICO (Lu et al., TMLR 2024): an automatically optimised prompt built from only 40 human-written examples lets GPT-3.5 evade six detectors, "decreasing their AUC by 0.5 on average", while keeping human-level readability. [peer-reviewed] - [SICO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10847)

Watermarking
- SynthID-Text was published in Nature vol. 634 (Oct 2024) (Dathathri et al.). - [Nature citation record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v634y2024i8035d10.1038_s41586-024-08025-4.html)
- Google DeepMind states SynthID text watermarking is in the Gemini app, works best on longer responses, is "less effective on responses to factual prompts" and that "confidence scores can be greatly reduced when an AI-generated text is thoroughly rewritten or translated". [vendor] - [DeepMind blog](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/watermarking-ai-generated-text-and-video-with-synthid/)
- Independent replication (arXiv 2609.09604, 2026, "Watermarks Without Verification") ran the open-source SynthID-Text on two open-weight models at a 1% FPR threshold: prose TPR 39% at 200 tokens and 56 to 59% at 400 tokens, full-length AUROC 0.78 and 0.82. It also notes Gemini has shipped the watermark since 2024 with no user opt-out, that Google's live comparison found no quality difference (win rates 0.501 and 0.515), that the scheme is "zero-bit", and that there is still no public detection API for outside parties. [preprint] - [arXiv 2609.09604](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09604)

Google Search's position
- Google Search Central (page last updated 2026-10-01): "using generative AI tools or other similar tools to generate many pages without adding value for users may violate Google's spam policy on scaled content abuse"; "It is critical to manually factcheck and review all AI-generated content"; "consider adding information on how your content was created". The page does not mention detecting AI content. [primary] - [Google: using gen-AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content)
- Search Quality Rater Guidelines, Jan 2025 update, section 4.6.6: a page whose main content is "copied, paraphrased, or generated by automation or AI" with "little to no effort, originality, or value" gets the Lowest rating; use of AI alone does not determine the rating. [secondary report of primary doc] - [Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/google-quality-raters-content-ai-generated-454161)
- Google spokespeople: Gary Illyes, "if you can maintain the quality of the content and the accuracy ... then technically it doesn't really matter"; Danny Sullivan, "We focus on the quality of content, not how content is produced." [secondary] - [Wordtracker summary](https://wordtracker.com/blog/content/google-responds-to-companies-using-ai-generated-content)

### Inferences
- The gap between vendor benchmark numbers (sub-1% FPR) and field data (9 to 15% flag rates on untouched human abstracts; 0 to 100% across 13 detectors on edited manuscripts) means a detector score should be treated as one noisy signal, never a pass/fail gate, for an editorial pipeline.
- Perplexity-based detectors are the ones most sensitive to the things a publisher can actually change (vocabulary richness, sentence-length variance, decoding temperature, paraphrase). Supervised classifiers like Pangram are trained on the lexical and structural fingerprints and are far harder to fool with light edits, but even Pangram's humaniser-robust model trades a 10% FPR for that robustness.
- For Google, the operative risk is the scaled-content-abuse policy and the "Lowest" rater label for low-effort pages, not detection per se. Evidence of effort, originality and manual fact-checking is what the published guidance asks for.
- Indian English matters here mainly through the non-native-writer bias: detectors penalise simpler, more predictable vocabulary (Liang 2023) and are confounded by editing style (Park 2026). Richer, more specific vocabulary lowers false positives rather than raising them.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the Nature SynthID-Text paper itself (paywall redirect); the per-length TPR figures above are from an independent 2026 replication on open-weight models, not from Google's Gemini deployment thresholds, which the replication says are unpublished.
- No study specifically measuring detector false positives on Indian English (as opposed to generic ESL/TOEFL or Korean/Chinese academic writers) was found.
- GPTZero's own current technical report with 2025-2026 numbers was not located; GPTZero figures above come from third-party studies and review sites.

## Q2. Lexical and structural tells: what is documented, by whom, and rule-ready lists

### Takeaway
The vocabulary excess is now quantified at corpus scale: Kobak et al. (Science Advances 2025) found 454 excess words in 2024 PubMed abstracts, almost all style words (66% verbs, 14% adjectives), with "delves" at 28x its expected frequency; Reinhart et al. (PNAS 2025) found GPT-4o uses "tapestry" 155x and "camaraderie" 162x more than humans and over-uses participial clauses 2 to 5x and nominalisations 1.5 to 2x. Wikipedia's field guide (updated Oct 2026) adds the structural catalogue (negative parallelism, copula avoidance, summary closers, bold-label lists, em dashes, title-case headings) and notes the word list drifts by model generation.

### Cited Findings

Corpus studies of vocabulary excess
- Kobak et al., Science Advances 11(27), 2 July 2025: 15M+ PubMed abstracts 2010 to 2024. 2024 showed 454 excess words (343 unique lemmas vs 180 in 2021 at the Covid peak); pre-Covid years had no word with excess gap > 0.01. Frequency ratios: "delves" r = 28.0, "underscores" 13.8, "showcasing" 10.7; excess gaps: "potential" 0.052, "findings" 0.041, "crucial" 0.037. Other excess words: pivotal, intricate, meticulous, realm, notably, comprehensive, across, additionally, surpassing. Covid-era excess was "almost entirely of content words"; 2024 excess "almost entirely of style words" (66% verbs, 14% adjectives). Lower bound of 13.5% of 2024 abstracts LLM-processed, reaching ~40% in subcorpora (China computational papers δ = 0.41; South Korea in Sensors δ = 0.34). [peer-reviewed] - [arXiv 2406.07016v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5); [Scimex summary of Science Advances paper](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/ai-chatbots-might-be-helping-to-write-more-than-1-in-10-biomedical-research-papers). Full word lists and code: [github.com/berenslab/llm-excess-vocab](https://github.com/berenslab/llm-excess-vocab)
- Liang et al., "Mapping the Increasing Use of LLMs in Scientific Papers" (2024): 950,965 papers Jan 2020 to Feb 2024 on arXiv, bioRxiv and Nature portfolio; Computer Science highest at up to 17.5% LLM-modified content, updated to up to 22% in the 2025 Nature Human Behaviour version. [peer-reviewed] - [arXiv 2404.01268](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01268); [Nature Human Behaviour 2025 PDF](https://nlp.stanford.edu/~manning/papers/Liang_et_al-2025-Nature_Human_Behaviour.pdf)

Grammatical and rhetorical tells (Biber features)
- Reinhart et al., PNAS 2025 ("Do LLMs write like humans?"), parallel human/LLM corpora with GPT-4o and Llama 3 variants. Instruction-tuned models over-use present participial clauses at 2 to 5x the human rate, nominalisations 1.5 to 2x, "that" clauses as subjects 2.6x (GPT-4o, d = 0.77), phrasal coordination 1.9x (GPT-4o, d = 0.81); GPT-4o uses more downtoners (barely, nearly) and agentless passives at about 50% of the human rate. Signature vocabulary over-represented 100x+: GPT-4o "camaraderie" (162x), "tapestry" (155x), "intricate" (119x), "underscore" (107x), "amidst" (100x); instruction-tuned Llama "unease", "palpable", "pang", "grapple", "vibrant". A random forest separated human from each LLM at 93 to 98% accuracy from these features alone. Key line: "Instruction tuning appears to make model output less human, not more." [peer-reviewed] - [arXiv 2410.16107v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2); [CMU news release](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2025/february/is-it-human-or-is-it-ai)

Human-expert clue taxonomy
- Russell et al. (ACL 2025): five "expert" annotators (frequent LLM users) hit 92.7% TPR / 3.3% FPR individually; their majority vote got 299 of 300 articles right and 100% under paraphrase and humanisation. Non-experts were at chance (56.7% TPR / 51.7% FPR). Clue types cited in expert explanations: vocabulary 53.1%, sentence structure 35.9%, grammar and punctuation 24.8%, originality 23.7%, quotes 22.3%, clarity 19.5%, formatting 15.0%, conclusions 13.1%, formality 12.3%, names and titles 11.7%, tone 9.3%, introductions 7.3%, factuality 7.2%, topics 3.1%. Specific tells: "vibrant", "crucial", "significantly", "testament", "beacon"; formulaic phrases ("as a beacon of hope and inspiration"); quote attributions using "notes" and "explains" where humans write "says"; generic invented names (Emily Carter, Sarah Thompson) and over-frequent "Dr." titles; "repetitive, optimistically vague summaries"; absence of contractions, filler and slang. Non-experts wrongly treated any fancy vocabulary and perfect grammar as AI. [peer-reviewed] - [Russell et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v1)

Wikipedia's field guide (WikiProject AI Cleanup)
- "Signs of AI writing" (last updated Oct 2026) is explicitly "descriptive, not prescriptive" and warns "Not all text featuring these indicators is AI-generated ... Many elements of AI writing can be found in editorials, blogs, or fan fiction." It gives vocabulary by era: 2023 to mid-2024: additionally, delve, crucial, intricate, meticulous, pivotal, tapestry, testament, underscore, vibrant; mid-2024 to mid-2025: align with, enhance, fostering, highlighting, showcasing; mid-2025 onward: emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing. It notes the distribution "is also somewhat different depending on ... LLM used" (Grok favours "causal", "empirical", "correlate"). [community guide] - [Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)
- Same page, content-level signs: undue significance ("stands as", "is a testament", "crucial role", "underscores importance", "reflects broader", "symbolizing", "key turning point", "indelible mark"); promotional travel-guide tone ("boasts", "vibrant", "rich", "profound", "nestled", "in the heart of", "groundbreaking", "diverse array"); superficial "-ing" analyses ("highlighting", "underscoring", "emphasizing", "contributing to"); vague association ("in connection with", "associated with", "linked to"); outline-like closers ("Despite its X, faces challenges ... future prospects"); near-ubiquitous "Awards and Recognition" or legacy sections. - [Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)
- Same page, grammar and style signs: copula avoidance ("serves as", "marks", "functions as", "represents", "features", "offers" replacing is/are, with "is/are" frequency down over 10% post-2022); three negative-parallelism types ("not only ... but also"; "It's not X, it's Y"; "Y rather than X"); over-use of em dashes (often spaced) replacing commas and semicolons; excessive boldface; inline-header vertical lists; title-case section headings; curly quotes; emoji as formatting; tables where prose would do; hedging disclaimers ("based on available information", "while specific information ... is limited"); knowledge-cutoff framing; filler ("It is important to note", "serves as a testament"); leftover placeholders ("[Your Name]", "2025-XX-XX", "INSERT_SOURCE_URL"); model-specific markup bugs (ChatGPT "[oaicite]", Gemini "[cite: 1]", Grok "grok_card", Perplexity "ppl-ai-file-upload"). The page also cites 2025 research that general humans perform "no better than random chance" while expert annotators hit 90% with 3.3% false positives. - [Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)

Em dashes and model-era drift
- On 14 Nov 2025 Sam Altman announced that ChatGPT now follows a custom instruction not to use em dashes, an implicit acknowledgement that em-dash overuse had become a recognised tell. [secondary news] - [Blockchain.news report](https://blockchain.news/ainews/chatgpt-custom-instructions-feature-delivers-improved-user-control-for-text-formatting)

Rule-ready consolidated list (each item traceable to a source above)
- Tier 1 single-word flags (Kobak r > 10 or Reinhart > 100x): delve/delves/delving, tapestry, camaraderie, underscore(s), showcasing/showcase, intricate, amidst, meticulous, pivotal, realm, testament, palpable, vibrant, beacon.
- Tier 2 excess words (Kobak, Wikipedia eras, Russell): crucial, potential, notably, comprehensive, additionally, surpassing, align with, enhance, fostering, highlighting, emphasizing, significantly, groundbreaking, profound, nestled, boasts, diverse array, rich (cultural heritage), enduring legacy, indelible mark, key turning point, plays a vital role, stands as, serves as.
- Phrase templates: "It is important to note", "In summary", "Overall", "In conclusion", "as a beacon of", "reflects broader", "in connection with", "Despite its ... faces challenges", "future prospects", "based on available information".
- Structural rules: (a) negative parallelism in all three forms; (b) copula replacement ("serves as", "functions as", "represents" where "is" fits); (c) sentences ending in a participial tail ("..., highlighting the importance of ..."); (d) nominalisation density above a corpus baseline; (e) em dashes per 1000 words above a human baseline, and any spaced em dash; (f) bold lead-ins on bullet items; (g) title-case H2/H3; (h) closing paragraph that restates rather than adds; (i) quote attribution verbs other than "said/says" dominating; (j) three-item lists repeated across paragraphs; (k) generic placeholder names and over-frequent honorifics.

### Inferences
- The word list is a moving target: Wikipedia's own era breakdown shows "delve/tapestry" fading after mid-2024 and "highlighting/showcasing/emphasizing" taking over, and Reinhart found instruction-tuned Llama has a different fingerprint from GPT-4o. Any rule set should be regenerated per model and per quarter from a frequency comparison against a human corpus (the Kobak method is simple: ratio of observed to expected frequency, and the repo is open).
- The grammatical tells (participial tails, nominalisations, copula avoidance, negative parallelism) are more durable than vocabulary because they come from instruction tuning rather than from any one word, and they are easy to check with a dependency parser.
- Human experts weight vocabulary (53%) and sentence structure (36%) above everything else, so a scorer that handles those two categories well covers the bulk of what skilled readers notice.

### Gaps
- No published 2026 study quantifies the specific vocabulary fingerprints of GPT-5-era, Claude 4/5 or Gemini 3.x models the way Kobak and Reinhart did for 2023 to 2024 models; Wikipedia's "mid-2025+" list is the most recent documented shift and is editor-observed, not measured.
- Paul Graham's often-quoted "delve" observation could not be located as a primary source in this search; his Oct 2024 essay "Writes and Write-Nots" is about writing as thinking, not about specific AI vocabulary. [secondary] - [Rey's summary](https://rey.bearblog.dev/write-nots/)
- No measured em-dash frequency comparison (human vs LLM, per 1000 words) was found; the em-dash tell rests on Wikipedia editor observation and Altman's statement.

## Q3. Measurable style metrics and open-source tooling

### Takeaway
Across the linguistic literature the discriminating features are consistent: humans show higher lexical diversity (unique words, lower sentence-to-sentence similarity), more proper nouns and nouns, greater variability of almost every feature across documents, simpler syntax (lower dependency depth) and more emotional intensity (especially negative); LLM text is longer-worded but structurally flatter. A 250-feature logistic model reaches 87% accuracy, Biber features reach 93 to 98%. Several free tools compute these (LFTK, pseudobibeR/Neurobiber, textstat, LexicalRichness, spaCy), and 2025 to 2026 saw dedicated rule linters for LLM tells (slopless, deslop for Vale, antislop).

### Cited Findings
- "Human Variability vs. Machine Consistency" (Dec 2024, M4 corpus: Wikipedia, WikiHow, arXiv, Reddit): 250 features via LFTK (247) plus dependency depth (spaCy), sentence-level cosine similarity and NRC emotion intensity. Humans wrote about 706 tokens vs 187 to 412 for LLMs; humans had lower syntactic depth than every LLM, more unique words with higher variability, lower sentence-to-sentence semantic similarity, and higher emotional intensity (especially negative). Logistic regression reached 87.15%; the top human-side features were total unique words, proper nouns per word, number of nouns and number of sentences; LLM text leaned on "structural features like spacing and reading time". Human variance collapses in rigid genres (PCA variance 4.70 for arXiv vs 296.75 for Wikipedia). [preprint] - [arXiv 2412.03025v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.03025v1)
- Lexical diversity (CogSci 2025 proceedings): ChatGPT 3.5, 4, o4-mini and 4.5 vs L1 and L2 human writers at four education levels; LLM texts "differed significantly from human-written texts for each variable measuring lexical diversity" across six dimensions (volume, abundance, variety-repetition, evenness, disparity, dispersion), showing less variation. [peer-reviewed proceedings] - [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/18n5k7c6)
- Reinhart (PNAS 2025) Biber-feature results (see Q2): participial clauses, nominalisations, that-clauses, phrasal coordination, downtoners, agentless passives; classifier 93 to 98%. [peer-reviewed] - [arXiv 2410.16107v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2). Feature extractors: pseudobibeR (R, CRAN) [tool] - [CRAN](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pseudobibeR/refman/pseudobibeR.html); Neurobiber, "fast and interpretable stylistic feature extraction" (2025) [preprint] - [arXiv 2502.18590](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18590v1)
- Detector-side metrics: GPTZero's public explanation is perplexity (predictability of each token) and burstiness (variance of sentence structure), with AI text scoring low on both. [secondary] - [fast.io](https://www.fast.io/resources/gptzero-ai-detector-review-2026/). Binoculars shows that a perplexity ratio normalised by a second model is a stronger signal than raw perplexity. [peer-reviewed] - [Binoculars](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.12070v3)
- Non-native bias explanation: "Non-native speakers often write with simpler, more predictable vocabulary ... their human-written text closely mimics the 'low perplexity' metrics". [peer-reviewed, via summary] - [Liang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819)
- Rule linters for LLM tells: slopless (Node/TypeScript, 50+ deterministic rules, no model calls) has rules named `llm-vocabulary`, `llm-openers`, `llm-disclaimer`, `response-wrapper`, plus narrative-slop metrics (body-action density, perception-verb density, clichés) and "academic paraphrasing artifacts"; its comparison table lists proselint (~30 rules, Python, general hygiene), write-good (~10, passive voice and weasel words), alex (~30, inclusive language), Vale (Go, requires a style-guide config) and textlint. It says proselint "predates the LLM-output era and has no rules specifically targeting LLM artifacts". [tool docs] - [slopless comparison wiki](https://github.com/berelevant-ai/slopless/wiki/Comparison); npm listing - [socket.dev slopless](https://socket.dev/npm/package/slopless/overview/0.2.35)
- deslop is a Vale style package flagging "stock phrases, hedges, and structural tics that mark machine-written text". [tool] - [deslop listing](https://tomevault.io/tome/JMill/deslop); antislop is a Rust crate for the same purpose. [tool] - [docs.rs antislop](https://docs.rs/crate/antislop/latest)
- General-purpose metric libraries (established tools): textstat (Flesch, Gunning Fog, etc.) - [github.com/textstat/textstat](https://github.com/textstat/textstat); LexicalRichness (TTR, MTLD, HD-D) - [github.com/LSYS/LexicalRichness](https://github.com/LSYS/LexicalRichness); spaCy (POS, dependency depth, NER) - [spacy.io](https://spacy.io); LanguageTool (grammar and style, supports en-IN variants) - [languagetool.org](https://languagetool.org); Vale - [vale.sh](https://vale.sh); proselint - [github.com/amperser/proselint](https://github.com/amperser/proselint)

### Inferences
- A practical "humanness" feature vector for a 900 to 1500 word article, grounded in the above: (1) sentence-length standard deviation and coefficient of variation (burstiness); (2) MTLD or HD-D rather than raw TTR because TTR is length-sensitive; (3) proper-noun and number density (named places, people, prices, dates); (4) mean sentence-to-sentence embedding cosine (lower is more human); (5) dependency depth (humans lower); (6) participial-tail rate and nominalisation rate per 100 sentences; (7) copula ratio (is/are vs "serves as" family); (8) paragraph-length variance; (9) first-person and contraction counts (Russell's "formality" clue); (10) tell-list hits per 1000 words. Flesch and Gunning Fog are weak discriminators on their own because LLM text is clearer, not harder, and Weisberg's comment (Q7) suggests clarity is not what readers penalise.
- Baselines must come from a matched human corpus in the same genre (sneaker and streetwear editorial), because the variability study shows human feature distributions shift dramatically by genre.
- Indian English context: spelling variants (colour, organise, programme) and local nouns (rupee prices, city names, festivals, cricket references) increase rare-word rate and proper-noun density, which moves text toward the human side of every metric above, so they are useful on both the metric and the reader axes.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly correlates these metrics with editorial quality ratings or with human preference for web articles; the metrics are validated as human-vs-machine discriminators, not as quality predictors.
- I found no published Indian English reference values (sentence-length variance, MTLD) for web editorial prose to use as baselines.

## Q4. Techniques that reduce machine signals: what the evidence says

### Takeaway
The strongest published effects come from rewriting rather than from generation settings: paraphrase (DIPPER) collapses zero-shot detection from 70% to under 5%, an optimised 40-example in-context prompt (SICO) cuts detector AUC by 0.5, and a single-user LoRA rewriter removes about 80 to 90% of configured tells while keeping BERTScore content similarity at 0.93 to 0.94. Critique-and-revise (Self-Refine) improves human preference by about 20 points but has no detector evidence attached. Decoding changes (sampling, repetition penalty) measurably confuse detectors (RAID). Human experts, however, still catch paraphrased and prompt-humanised text at 100%, so these techniques beat classifiers more reliably than they beat skilled readers.

### Cited Findings
- Paraphrase: DIPPER (11B paraphraser with lexical-diversity and reordering controls) drops DetectGPT from 70.3% to 4.6% at 1% FPR "without appreciably modifying the input semantics", and evades watermarking, GPTZero and OpenAI's classifier. [peer-reviewed] - [DIPPER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13408)
- Few-shot style prompting: SICO builds an evasion prompt from 40 human examples and "a limited number of LLM inferences", cutting six detectors' AUC by 0.5 on average (about 0.4 in most cases), with "human-level readability and task completion"; the effect transfers across proxy detectors (GPT-3.5 classifier, GPTZero, DetectGPT). [peer-reviewed] - [SICO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10847)
- Prompt-based "humanisation" by a frontier model: in Russell et al., o1-Pro with anti-detection prompting reduced Binoculars to 6.7% TPR and Fast-DetectGPT to 23.3%, but Pangram Humanizers kept 96.7% TPR (at 10% FPR) and the expert majority vote stayed at 100% TPR / 0% FPR. GPT-4o paraphrasing left experts at 100%. [peer-reviewed] - [Russell et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v1)
- Decoding: RAID shows detectors are fooled by "variations in sampling strategies, repetition penalties", i.e. higher-temperature or penalised sampling alone shifts detector output. [peer-reviewed] - [RAID](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07940)
- Vocabulary enrichment: prompting ChatGPT to "enrich vocabulary" reduced false-positive classification of human essays, while simplifying increased it, demonstrating that perplexity-style detectors respond to lexical richness. [peer-reviewed] - [Liang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819)
- Critique-and-revise: Self-Refine (same model generates, critiques, revises) was "preferred by humans and automatic metrics ... improving by ~20% absolute on average" across 7 tasks with GPT-3.5, ChatGPT and GPT-4. [peer-reviewed, NeurIPS 2023] - [Self-Refine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651)
- Sentence-level tell removal via a small LoRA rewriter (InMyStyle, CIIS 2026): on general-domain demos the AI input carried 11.7 configured tells per text; the 0.5B/1.5B/3B/7B Qwen2.5 adapters cut this to 2.7/1.3/2.0/1.0 (AI-tell reduction 0.76 to 0.91) while BERTScore content F1 stayed at 0.93 to 0.94 and output length stayed at 0.96 to 1.01 of input. Five LLM judges rated the rewrites 22% lower on perceived AI-ness than the inputs. The authors stress "Our goal is not to evade AI-text detectors" and that gains "favor content-preserving naturalization more than recovery of personal style". [peer-reviewed conference preprint] - [arXiv 2607.29238](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29238)
- What experts still catch after humanisation: homogeneous quotes (22.3% of expert clues), generic names and titles (11.7%), vague optimistic conclusions (13.1%), lack of originality (23.7%) and factual slips (7.2%). [peer-reviewed] - [Russell et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v1)
- Detector defence to be aware of: retrieval over a provider's own generation history detects 80 to 97% of DIPPER-paraphrased text at 1% FPR, so provider-side logging could in principle beat paraphrase even where classifiers fail. [peer-reviewed] - [DIPPER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13408)

### Inferences
- Ordering by evidence strength for lowering detector scores: (1) paraphrase or rewrite pass with lexical-diversity control; (2) an optimised few-shot style prompt built from real human articles (SICO shows 40 examples suffice); (3) a small per-voice LoRA rewriter; (4) decoding changes (cheap but shallow); (5) critique-and-revise loops (quality gains, unproven on detectors).
- Ordering for convincing skilled human readers (Russell's taxonomy): remove tell vocabulary and uniform sentence templates first, then fix quotes (use real, attributed, "said"-style quotes), use real named entities and specific numbers, write a conclusion that adds rather than restates, allow contractions and first person where the brand voice permits.
- "Deliberate imperfection" (typos, run-ons) has no supporting study; the Russell paper actually shows experts treat run-ons as human but non-experts treat perfect grammar as AI, and Karr 2026 shows humanisers that introduce errors still get flagged in some settings. Not recommended without evidence.
- Because Pangram-class classifiers survive prompt humanisation, a pipeline that aims only at "beating detectors" is chasing a moving target; aiming at the human-expert clue list also moves classifier scores because both are driven by the same lexical and structural features.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found that measures Self-Refine or Reflexion-style loops against AI-text detectors or against human "sounds like AI" ratings.
- No study was found isolating the effect of injecting retrieved real quotes and data on detector scores; the only evidence is indirect (expert clue categories).
- No measured evidence for "persona plus constraints" prompting on long-form web articles specifically; SICO is the closest (in-context examples optimised for evasion).

## Q5. Fine-tuning versus prompting versus prompt optimisation for voice

### Takeaway
LoRA fine-tuning on roughly 80k tokens per author (StyleTunedLM) beats 5- and 10-shot prompting on authorship accuracy (0.879 vs 0.693/0.680) and lexical alignment, and a 2026 single-user study shows 487 paragraphs (about 36 papers) are enough to train a 0.5B to 7B rewriter whose quality plateaus regardless of size. The documented risks are content memorisation (fixed by masking named entities), degraded instruction following (fixed by merging an instruction adapter) and, when fine-tuning introduces facts the base model does not know, a linear increase in hallucination (Gekhman et al.). DSPy-style optimisation has strong task-accuracy evidence but no published style or voice evaluation.

### Cited Findings
- StyleTunedLM (Sept 2024): LLaMA-2-7b, LoRA, lr 5e-5, 3 epochs, max length 256, about 80,000 tokens per author from ten Project Gutenberg authors. Authorship-classifier accuracy 0.879 (fine-tuned) vs 0.693 (5-shot), 0.680 (10-shot), 0.263 (instruct baseline); lexical error MSE 1.39 vs 3.80 / 3.31 / 2.67; syntactic JSD 0.06 vs 0.07 / 0.06 / 0.15; average perplexity reduction 7.0%. Instruction following preserved by merging the style LoRA with a LoRA trained on LIMA; content memorisation (character names) controlled by masking spaCy-detected person entities from loss, which "has minimal impact on style learning". Abstract notes the model "struggles with content memorization". [preprint] - [arXiv 2409.04574v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04574v1); [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04574)
- InMyStyle (CIIS 2026, Oct 2026): single author, 36 papers (2012 to 2025) → 487 prose paragraphs → 1,461 AI-shadow/original pairs via three local helper models (Qwen2.5-3B, Llama-3.2-3B, Phi-3-mini); split 414/73 paragraphs; Qwen2.5 0.5B/1.5B/3B/7B with LoRA rank 8, alpha 16, dropout 0.05, all linear layers, 3 epochs, lr 2e-4, effective batch 16, 4-bit NF4 QLoRA, response-only loss. Composite score plateaued at 0.689 to 0.695 across all sizes and decoding modes; 3B matched 7B within 0.004 with 47% less training time. Authorship probability stayed at 0.51 to 0.53 and stylometric improvement near zero, so personal voice was not recovered; perceived-AI judge scores fell with size (6.24, 5.80, 4.93, 4.45 on a 0-10 scale) vs human paragraphs 3.47 and raw generations 8.44. "The study does not establish generalization across users." [peer-reviewed conference preprint] - [arXiv 2607.29238](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29238)
- Hallucination risk: Gekhman et al. (EMNLP 2024) show fine-tuning examples that introduce knowledge the model lacks "are learned significantly slower" and, once learned, "linearly increase the model's tendency to hallucinate"; LLMs "mostly acquire factual knowledge through pre-training, whereas fine-tuning teaches them to use it more efficiently". [peer-reviewed] - [arXiv 2405.05904](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05904)
- Knowledge-mismatch hypothesis: small models fine-tuned on outputs of larger models "produce more wrong answers on questions that have not been seen before". [preprint] - [arXiv 2411.00878](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.00878)
- Small-data limits: a 2025 small-model study found that with 500 to 2,000 records, raising LoRA rank/alpha to 64/40 did not beat the base model, the model adopted "tone and poetic style" but not exact phrasing, and 7,000 records clearly beat 800. [preprint] - [KnowsLM, arXiv 2504.04569](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.04569)
- Provider fine-tuning: OpenAI's supervised fine-tuning guide says the minimum is 10 examples, that "improvements from fine-tuning on 50 to 100 examples" are typical, and to "start with 50 well-crafted demonstrations"; the same documentation now states OpenAI is winding down the fine-tuning platform for new users. [vendor docs] - [OpenAI supervised fine-tuning](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/supervised-fine-tuning)
- Prompt optimisation: DSPy reports compiled pipelines beating few-shot and expert prompts by 25 to 65% on reasoning benchmarks (up to 46% on GSM8K) and offers BootstrapFinetune to distil optimised prompts into weights; none of the cited material evaluates writing style or voice. [secondary summary of DSPy paper] - [Emergent Mind DSPy topic](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/dspy); the DSPy paper itself - [arXiv 2310.03714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03714)
- Related adapter approaches for voice: StyleRemix (LoRA modules per interpretable style axis), TinyStyler (800M model + authorship embeddings for few-shot transfer) and AuthorMix (author LoRAs composed layer by layer) are summarised in the InMyStyle related-work section. - [arXiv 2607.29238](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29238)

### Inferences
- For a 50 to 300 article corpus (roughly 50k to 400k tokens), the evidence supports a LoRA on a 3B to 8B open model for voice, with three guardrails taken directly from the papers: mask named entities and numbers from the loss (StyleTunedLM) so the adapter learns style not facts; merge with or regularise against an instruction adapter so the model still follows briefs; and keep factual content outside the fine-tune (retrieval or source notes in the prompt) because Gekhman shows fine-tuning on unfamiliar facts increases hallucination.
- InMyStyle's result that authorship probability never moved off 0.51 to 0.53 suggests a single-user LoRA on a few hundred paragraphs reliably delivers "naturalisation" (tell removal) but not a recognisable personal voice; voice may need more data, author-specific pairs, or a few-shot layer on top.
- Curated style guides plus exemplar prompting are the cheapest baseline and SICO shows 40 examples are enough to shift detectors; StyleTunedLM shows prompting caps authorship accuracy near 0.68 to 0.69 where fine-tuning reaches 0.88. The practical ladder is: style guide → optimised few-shot exemplars → LoRA rewriter as a final pass.
- Provider fine-tuning is becoming less available (OpenAI wind-down), which favours open-weight LoRA via Together, Fireworks or local training.

### Gaps
- No sourced cost figures for LoRA training on a 7B model or for Gemini/Together/Fireworks fine-tuning were gathered in this pass.
- No head-to-head study of DSPy (or similar prompt optimisers) against fine-tuning on a style or voice metric was found.
- Anthropic, OpenAI and Hugging Face cookbooks on style fine-tuning were not reviewed here.

## Q6. Commercial "humanizer" tools

### Takeaway
Independent 2025 to 2026 evidence agrees on two things: humanizers do defeat most detectors (Undetectable AI v11 pushed detection on AI text below 4%; StealthGPT pushed GPTZero's miss rate above 50%), and the one classifier trained specifically against them (Pangram's humaniser model, built from a 19-tool study) keeps detecting at the cost of a higher false-positive rate. Quality findings on these tools come almost entirely from review sites with commercial conflicts, which report grammar errors and meaning drift; no peer-reviewed quality evaluation was found.

### Cited Findings
- DAMAGE (Masrour, Emi, Spero, Jan 2025; the authors are Pangram staff): studied "19 AI humanizer and paraphrasing tools", found "many existing AI detectors fail to detect humanized text", and trained a detector with "data-centric augmentation" that shows "sufficient cross-humanizer generalization to remain robust to adaptive attacks". [preprint, vendor-affiliated] - [arXiv 2501.03437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03437v1)
- Karr et al. 2026: after Undetectable AI v11 humanisation, "fewer than 4% of AI-labeled rewrites remain flagged (post-humanization detection rate < 4%; FNR > 96%)" across commercial detectors, so "honest AI-editing results in a higher sanction risk than humanizer-assisted evasion". The paper's Table 8 reports pooled linguistic shifts caused by the humaniser. [preprint] - [arXiv 2608.11256](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11256)
- BFI (Jabarian and Imas 2025): StealthGPT made GPTZero lose "its capacity to detect AI-generated text, showing false negative rate scores around 50% and above across most genres and LLM models", while Pangram's FNR "is robust to the use of current 'humanizers'". [working paper] - [BFI brief](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Artificial-Writing-and-Automated-Detection.pdf)
- Russell et al.: experts caught 100% of prompt-humanised articles; the clues that survived humanisation were misused honorifics ("Dr.", "Prof." at unusually high frequency), homogeneous quotes and generic names. [peer-reviewed] - [Russell et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v1)
- Review-site quality claims (all non-peer-reviewed and from parties selling detectors or competing humanizers): Undetectable.ai "scored 87% for meaning preservation" but with "lower readability and visible output errors" and outputs that "contained erroneous elements that were out of context" [secondary, detector vendor] - [Winston AI](https://gowinston.ai/best-ai-humanizer-tool/); Undetectable.ai "compromises writing quality (even coherence) to evade detection, and yet still isn't foolproof" [secondary] - [EssayDone review](https://www.essaydone.ai/tools-review/undetectable-ai-review.html); StealthGPT output "contained numerous grammar mistakes and was of such poor quality that it would be unusable" in one test [secondary, competing humanizer] - [Phrasly](https://phrasly.ai/blog/stealthgpt-review-does-it-work), yet another reviewer claims it "maintained 95% of original meaning while improving average readability by 12%" [secondary] - [aixradar](https://aixradar.com/stealthgpt-review/). StealthGPT's own blog claims a perfect score in a 25-tool test. [vendor] - [StealthGPT](https://www.stealthgpt.ai/blog/ai-humanizer-test-stealthgpt-perfect-score)

### Inferences
- The mechanism behind degradation is visible in the academic results: humanizers optimise against perplexity-type signals by substituting rarer words and scrambling syntax (Karr's "linguistic shifts"), which is exactly the kind of lexical and syntactic noise that reduces semantic fidelity and introduces grammar errors; they do not add information, specificity or voice.
- For a publisher, humanizers are the wrong tool on three counts: Google's guidance targets low-effort scaled content regardless of detector scores; Pangram-class detectors are trained against the 19 main tools; and expert readers still identify the output. Any detector gains are temporary.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study measuring meaning preservation, grammaticality or reader preference for Undetectable.ai, StealthGPT, Humanize AI, WriteHuman or Surfer Humanizer output was found; all quality numbers above are from conflicted review sites and contradict each other.
- DAMAGE's full list of the 19 tools and per-tool bypass rates were not extractable from the abstract page.

## Q7. Reader-side evidence: do readers notice or engage differently?

### Takeaway
Blind, readers do not reliably tell AI from human articles (40 to 52% identification) and often rate AI text equal or slightly higher on quality and engagement; labelled, they penalise AI authorship and say they disengage. The best 2026 experiments (Scientific Reports, Journal of Decision Making) agree on this pattern. No field data on dwell time, scroll depth or shares for AI vs human web articles was found.

### Cited Findings
- Villanova University study, Journal of Decision Making 2026 (DOI 10.1017/jdm.2026.10042): 1,682 participants rated six short stories; AI stories rated 6% higher on quality and 8% higher on engagement; readers correctly identified AI authorship only 40 to 52% of the time in two follow-ups (400+ participants each); stories believed to be human got a further ~3% boost regardless of true origin, which the authors call "a bias towards narratives written by real people". Senior author Deena Weisberg: human stories "might have simply challenged readers more" since "AI writing tends to be clearer, more direct and easier to process". [peer-reviewed, via press] - [Time, 4 Aug 2026](https://time.com/article/2026/08/04/is-ai-telling-better-stories-than-us/)
- Gilardi et al. (University of Zurich), Scientific Reports, 24 Aug 2026: 599 German-speaking Swiss participants; human-written vs ChatGPT-rewritten (AI-assisted) vs fully AI-generated news. Perceived credibility, readability and expertise did not differ (all p > .45). After disclosure, both AI conditions showed higher willingness to continue reading (d = 0.53 to 0.61) but no change in future willingness to read AI news; authors interpret this as "curiosity-driven engagement rather than endorsement". [peer-reviewed] - [arXiv 2409.03500v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.03500v4); [Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-58743-0)
- Bynder (vendor survey, April 2024, 2,000 US/UK consumers, two 300-word car-cleaning articles): 56% preferred the ChatGPT version blind; 50% could identify the AI copy; 52% say they become less engaged when they suspect AI; 26% would see the brand as impersonal and 20% as lazy if website copy does not feel human. [vendor] - [Bynder press release](https://www.bynder.com/en/press-media/ai-vs-human-made-content-study/)
- Reuters Institute 2025 Digital News Report: only 12% comfortable with fully AI-generated news, 21% when a human controls the process, up to 43% when a journalist leads with AI support, 62% prefer entirely human-made news. [secondary summary of primary report] - [Twipe summary](https://www.twipemobile.com/what-the-2025-digital-news-report-tells-us-about-ai-in-news/); [journalism.co.uk](https://www.journalism.co.uk/what-the-public-wants-seven-opportunities-for-newsrooms-in-the-age-of-generative-ai/)
- Expert vs lay detection: Russell et al. show lay readers at chance (56.7% TPR / 51.7% FPR) but heavy LLM users at 92.7% / 3.3%. [peer-reviewed] - [Russell et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v1)

### Inferences
- The commercial risk is not that typical readers detect AI prose; it is that a growing minority of heavy-LLM-using readers (the Russell "experts", likely over-represented among sneaker-forum and tech-adjacent audiences) do detect it, and that a label or a strong suspicion triggers the stated disengagement and "lazy brand" perception.
- Because blind quality ratings are flat or favour the clearer AI text, "humanising" should not mean making text harder to read; it should mean adding the signals readers associate with human effort (specific detail, real quotes, opinion, local context) while keeping clarity.

### Gaps
- No 2025 to 2026 study of behavioural engagement metrics (dwell time, scroll depth, shares, return visits) comparing AI and human web articles in the wild was found; all evidence is self-report in experiments or surveys.
- No India-specific reader study was found.

## Q8. LLM-as-judge for editorial quality and how to build a calibrated "humanness and voice" scorer

### Takeaway
GPT-4-class judges agree with humans about 80% of the time on general chat quality (MT-Bench), but a June 2026 systematic audit finds the dominant judge bias today is style (judges prefer markdown over plain prose 73 to 97% of the time where humans prefer it only 57%), verbosity bias varies by model (Llama +0.44, Gemini Pro +0.40, Claude Sonnet 4 -0.12), position bias is now negligible (≤ 0.04), and the best mitigation (combined chain-of-thought plus rubric with position swap) lifts human agreement to about 70% (κ ≈ 0.55). Judges also disagree with each other on "AI-ness" and score genuine human prose at 3.5/10 AI-ness, so a judge needs calibration against a small human-labelled set and should be paired with deterministic metrics.

### Cited Findings
- Zheng et al. (NeurIPS 2023, MT-Bench): "strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement", the same as human-human agreement; biases identified: position, verbosity, self-enhancement, limited reasoning; mitigations: swapping positions, few-shot examples, chain-of-thought, reference-guided grading. [peer-reviewed] - [arXiv 2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)
- Soumik, "Judging the Judges" (arXiv 2604.23178v2, June 2026): five judges from four families (Gemini 2.5 Pro and Flash, Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-4o, Llama 3.3-70B), MT-Bench (n = 400 human-labelled pairs) and LLMBar adversarial pairs, nine mitigation strategies. Style bias "is the dominant bias (0.10 to 0.76 baseline across all models, mostly favoring markdown over plain prose)": humans preferred the markdown side 57% of the time while four of five judges preferred it 73 to 97% (GPT-4o 53%, within 3 pp of humans). Position bias ≤ 0.04 for all models. Length-aware verbosity bias: Llama +0.44, Gemini Pro +0.40, Gemini Flash +0.24, GPT-4o -0.04, Claude Sonnet 4 -0.12 (prefers concise); on truncation controls all judges correctly prefer the complete answer (0.88 to 1.00). Best configuration: "Combined Budget" (single merged CoT + 5-criterion rubric prompt, called twice with positions swapped, tie on disagreement) with Gemini 2.5 Flash reached 71.0% human agreement, κ = 0.549, at about $0.001 per evaluation; Claude Sonnet 4 with the same strategy 69.5% at about $0.015. Debiasing gains that survive correction: Claude +11.5 pp, Flash +7.5 pp, Claude CoT +7.3 pp. "We do not see any strategy that systematically reduces verbosity bias across all models; this remains an open problem." Position swap hurts on adversarial LLMBar by 4+ pp. [preprint] - [arXiv 2604.23178](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178)
- Judges as AI-ness raters (InMyStyle 2026): five chat judges (ChatGPT 5.6, Claude Sonnet 5, Gemini 3.6 Flash, Grok 4.5, Mistral) gave 400 ratings on a 0-10 AI-ness scale; pooled means were human 3.47, AI-shadow 6.85, InMyStyle rewrite 5.34, raw generation 8.44, with "a fair amount of calibration discrepancy" between judges. [peer-reviewed conference preprint] - [arXiv 2607.29238](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29238)
- Prompted LLMs as detectors (Russell et al.): GPT-4o with zero-shot + CoT + the expert guidebook reached 78.0% TPR / 10.7% FPR; o1 54.0% / 4.0%; better than Binoculars and RADAR, worse than experts and Pangram; authors suggest "fine-tuning may be helpful". [peer-reviewed] - [Russell et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v1)
- Self-preference: judges score their own and same-family outputs higher (Panickssery et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024), as cited and re-measured with a round-robin design in Soumik 2026. - [arXiv 2604.23178](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23178)
- Human agreement ceiling: two humans agree about 80% of the time on MT-Bench-style preferences, so 80% is the realistic target for any judge. - [arXiv 2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)

### Inferences
- Scorer architecture supported by the evidence: Layer 1, deterministic metrics (Q3 feature vector plus Q2 tell rules) computed with spaCy/LFTK/LexicalRichness and a tell linter such as slopless or a Vale/deslop ruleset, normalised against a matched human corpus of sneaker and streetwear articles; Layer 2, a Binoculars-style perplexity ratio from two small open models as a cheap continuous signal (knowing it over-flags memorised and simple text); Layer 3, an LLM judge using a merged CoT + rubric prompt, run pairwise against a human reference article with positions swapped, on a model with low verbosity and style bias (Claude Sonnet 4 or GPT-4o per Soumik), with markdown stripped from both texts before judging to neutralise style bias.
- Rubric criteria should mirror what expert humans actually use (Russell): vocabulary tells, sentence-template repetition, quote realism and attribution verbs, specificity (names, numbers, places), originality of argument, conclusion that adds, register (contractions, first person), plus a factuality check against the brief. Avoid criteria that reward length or structure.
- Calibration recipe grounded in the papers: label 100 to 400 article pairs with two humans (MT-Bench used 400 pairs and reports ±0.05 bootstrap CIs at that size), measure judge agreement and κ, pick the judge-strategy pair with the best agreement, then re-validate quarterly because the InMyStyle data show judges drift and disagree on AI-ness. Use the human set to set the threshold on the Layer 1 and 2 scores as well.
- Expect a well-built judge to reach about 70 to 80% agreement with editors, not more; combine with the deterministic layer for the final gate and route disagreements to a human.

### Gaps
- No published rubric or benchmark specifically for "voice consistency" or "humanness" of web editorial prose with inter-rater agreement numbers was found; the agreement figures above are for general chat quality (MT-Bench) and for human-vs-AI detection.
- No study was found on how many human-labelled examples are needed to calibrate a judge for a narrow editorial domain; the 100 to 400 range is an inference from MT-Bench and Soumik's sample sizes.
